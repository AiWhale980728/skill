# Evidence and Assessment

Use this reference when researching a domain, designing depth questions, evaluating learner answers, or deciding whether understanding transfers.

## Evidence protocol

### Decide when research is required

Research before teaching when claims are:

- current or likely to change
- niche or unfamiliar
- disputed
- numerically precise
- consequential for health, law, finance, safety, or public decisions
- dependent on a named paper, standard, dataset, policy, or source

Stable foundational concepts may be explained from existing knowledge, but do not attach invented citations.

### Prefer sources in this order

Adapt the hierarchy to the field:

1. Primary evidence: original research, official data, standards, statutes, specifications, archival material, or first-party documentation.
2. Authoritative synthesis: systematic reviews, major textbooks, professional bodies, or established reference works.
3. Credible expert interpretation: domain specialists who show sources and reasoning.
4. General explanation: reputable educational or journalistic material.

Use lower levels for orientation, not as substitutes for available primary evidence.

### Classify claims

Label important statements internally or explicitly as:

| Class | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Established fact | Strong, convergent support; definitions and scope are clear |
| Supported interpretation | Evidence is substantial but the conclusion involves modeling or judgment |
| Disputed position | Serious disagreement remains about evidence, assumptions, values, or definitions |
| Open question | Evidence cannot yet resolve the issue |
| Inference | A reasoned conclusion drawn from sources rather than directly stated by them |

Explain why a dispute persists. Common causes include insufficient data, measurement limits, different definitions, incompatible assumptions, value conflicts, or evidence that applies only under different conditions.

### Avoid false authority

- Never describe a position as expert consensus without adequate support.
- Present the strongest defensible version of competing positions.
- Do not invent a second side merely to make a symmetrical debate.
- State when a field has multiple schools rather than a single expert method.
- Separate the learner's goal from claims about what the field considers important.
- Cite sources close to the claims they support when citations are available.

## Depth-question design

Build a varied set instead of repeating "why?" questions.

### Explanation

Ask the learner to reconstruct an idea without borrowing the original phrasing.

Good: "How would you explain this mechanism to someone who understands the prerequisites but not this domain?"

### Discrimination

Expose concepts that look similar but behave differently.

Good: "Under what conditions would A and B lead to different predictions?"

### Connection

Test whether the learner can traverse the domain map.

Good: "How does this assumption affect the later model, and what breaks if it is removed?"

### Boundary

Reveal scope and hidden conditions.

Good: "Where does this model stop being useful, and how would you notice?"

### Counterexample

Test whether the learner can revise rather than defend a memorized rule.

Good: "What case appears to contradict the rule? Does it refute the rule or expose a missing condition?"

### Transfer

Apply the structure in an unfamiliar setting.

Good: "Use the model on this new case. Which facts matter, which do not, and what remains uncertain?"

### Evidence judgment

Test calibrated confidence.

Good: "What evidence would change your conclusion, and what evidence would not be decisive?"

## Answer assessment

Evaluate the response, not the learner.

| Level | Observable evidence | Best next move |
|---|---|---|
| Robust understanding | Accurate reconstruction, meaningful connections, boundaries, counterexamples, or successful transfer | Add one deeper implication, then advance |
| Working understanding | Core idea is sound but conditions, precision, or connections are incomplete | Ask one targeted question and request a revised explanation |
| Fragmented understanding | Correct phrases appear, but relationships are confused or copied | Change the representation, contrast concepts, or use a concrete case |
| Foundational gap | The answer depends on a missing prerequisite or is unrelated | Teach the prerequisite directly, then retry a simpler transfer |
| Unknown | The learner says they do not know | Normalize it, provide a foothold, and ask a smaller question |

Do not infer mastery from agreement, familiarity, fluency, or a single correct answer.

## Completion test

Consider a concept usable when the learner can demonstrate most of the following at an appropriate level:

- explain it accurately in original language
- connect it to prerequisites and consequences
- distinguish it from a nearby concept
- state a boundary or condition
- handle a counterexample
- transfer it to a new case
- calibrate confidence to the evidence

Consider the accelerated learning cycle complete when the learner can explain the domain's structure, use its central models on a new problem, distinguish consensus from dispute, and name remaining uncertainty. This is a milestone, not a claim of expertise.
